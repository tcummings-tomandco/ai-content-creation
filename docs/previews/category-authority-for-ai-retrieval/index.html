How do you build category authority so AI tools retrieve your brand?

Category authority is what happens when your brand becomes the default answer AI tools give for a whole category, not just for its own name. It's built by earning citations, structured data and mentions across trusted third-party platforms (comparison sites, review platforms, trade bodies) that large language models draw on. Ofcom's 2026 research suggests around 71% of UK adults now see an AI-generated answer at least sometimes, so staying off those platforms means staying out of most category conversations.

What is the category authority play, and why does it matter now?

Think of it as share of voice, but for AI answers instead of search rankings. Google measured how often you appeared in ten blue links. AI tools measure something else: how often they retrieve you, alongside independent sources, when someone asks a question about your category rather than your company.

That distinction matters because most category questions never mention a brand by name. Someone asking an AI tool "what's the best accounting software for a UK sole trader" or "which comparison site should I use for van insurance" is asking about a category. Whether you show up depends on whether trusted sources already associate your name with that category.

Adoption is moving fast enough that this isn't a future problem. 54% of UK adults now use an AI tool such as ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, Ofcom found in its 2026 Adults' Media Use and Attitudes research, up from 31% the year before. Uptake skews young (79% of 16 to 24 year olds use one, against 18% of over-65s), but the trend line only points one way.

Trust hasn't caught up with usage. 57% of adults who know what AI is say they'd trust an AI-written summary less than a human-written one, per the same Ofcom report. That gap matters for category authority specifically: an AI tool that's cautious about its own credibility leans harder on citing sources it judges independently reliable, not on its own paraphrase.

Tom & Co analysis of Ofcom's 2026 Adults' Media Use and Attitudes research: multiplying the 94% of UK adults with home internet access by the 75% of online adults who read AI-generated search summaries at least sometimes puts roughly 71% of the whole UK adult population within reach of an AI-generated answer, a figure Ofcom's own report doesn't state directly.

That's not a niche audience. It's most of the UK adult population, at least some of the time. A brand with no presence in the sources an AI tool actually pulls from is, in practice, invisible to a huge and growing share of category research.

Marketing teams have noticed. Tom & Co analysis of British Chambers of Commerce and gov.uk data puts the number of UK businesses now using AI for marketing or content work at roughly 2.1 million. Most of that effort still goes into a business's own website and social channels, the layer an AI tool trusts least when it's answering a category question.

Why isn't good on-site content enough to get your brand cited?

Publishing a brilliant page on your own site is necessary, but it's not sufficient. Large language models blend what you say about yourself with what independent, trusted sources say about you, and they weight the second kind more heavily precisely because you didn't write it.

Researchers at Princeton and IIT Delhi tested this directly. Their Generative Engine Optimization study, published at the 2024 ACM SIGKDD conference, found that adding citations, statistics and named expert attribution to a page lifted its citation rate in AI-generated answers by up to 40%. The tactics that worked best made a claim independently checkable, not just more persuasive.

Here's the bit that trips brands up. The paper measured what happens to a page once it's optimised, but it says nothing about whether an AI tool ever sees your page in the first place. That's a retrieval problem, and retrieval is decided by the wider web, not by you.

For most UK categories, that wider web runs through a small set of intermediaries: comparison sites, review platforms, trade bodies and structured data providers. That's the shift most GEO advice misses. It obsesses over your own page and ignores the ecosystem an AI tool actually trusts more.

Picture two UK accountancy software brands. One has a slick website and nothing else. The other has a modest website, forty verified reviews on a named comparison platform, a Wikipedia mention inside an "accounting software" category page, and a techUK membership listing. An AI tool answering "best accounting software UK" has far more independently verifiable material to draw on for the second brand.

Which UK platforms actually shape what AI tools say about your category?

Five kinds of UK platform turn up again and again in category-level AI answers. None of them are new inventions built for the AI era, they're the same intermediaries that shaped SEO and word of mouth for two decades, just doing a new job now.

UK price and product comparison sites

Compare the Market, MoneySuperMarket, GoCompare and Confused.com sit at the top of most UK insurance and utilities categories, and the Competition and Markets Authority's own market study into these digital comparison tools, published 26 September 2017, found them heavily used and, on balance, good for UK consumers shopping around. That history of scrutiny is exactly what makes them trusted enough for an AI tool to lean on.

B2B review and software platforms

For B2B categories the equivalent layer is software and service review sites. A UK accountancy tech buyer researching options is far more likely to land on a named review platform's comparison page than on any single vendor's site, so a category-level presence there matters more than another feature page at home.

Trade bodies and professional associations

Membership of a recognised trade body such as techUK, or a sector-specific professional association, signals something an AI tool can weight: a third party has already checked you belong in this category. It's a slower route to authority than a comparison listing, but it's a much harder one to fake.

Wikipedia, Wikidata and structured data

Wikipedia and its structured sibling Wikidata still feed a surprising amount of what AI tools treat as settled fact about a category or a company. Most brands don't qualify for a standalone Wikipedia page, and trying to force one is a fast way to get it deleted, but appearing accurately inside a category or industry page is realistic and worth doing properly.

Trade press and digital PR

Trade press coverage works differently to a listing. It's dated, third-party, and hard to buy outright, which is exactly why AI tools weight it heavily. A single well-placed piece in a named UK trade title, referencing a specific statistic or case study, can outweigh months of unlinked blog posts on your own site.

How do you actually earn a place on those platforms?

None of this happens by accident. Each platform type above has its own process, and skipping the boring admin is the single biggest reason brands with genuinely good products stay invisible to category-level AI answers.

  1. Claim every comparison and review listing that already exists for you. Most brands have an unclaimed or half-complete profile sitting on at least one comparison site already.
  2. Build a genuine review-generation habit, not a one-off push. A trickle of dated, verified reviews reads as more current and more trustworthy than a spike from a single campaign.
  3. Add schema.org structured data to your own site. Organisation, Product and Review markup gives AI crawlers a machine-readable version of the same facts a comparison site holds.
  4. Apply for relevant trade body and association membership. It's slow, but it's a credential a competitor can't simply copy.
  5. Correct factual errors on Wikipedia and Wikidata where they exist. Do it transparently, with sources, and never write about your own company as though you were a neutral editor.

None of these five steps is a quick win. Comparison sites index new listings on their own schedule, trade bodies review applications quarterly, and Wikipedia edits get reverted if they look remotely promotional. Budget months, not days.

Some of this is fast. Claiming an existing comparison listing can take twenty minutes. Some of it is slow. A trade body application or a first Wikipedia mention can take a full quarter. Do the fast ones this month and start the slow ones in parallel, not after.

A UK van insurance broker that spent one afternoon completing its Compare the Market and Confused.com profiles, rather than writing another blog post, is taking a more direct route to being the broker an AI tool names when someone asks for the best van insurance comparison in the UK, because that's genuinely where the comparison happens.

Does this replace ranking well in ChatGPT or optimising your own site?

No. Category authority is the foundation the other two GEO plays sit on top of. How do you rank in ChatGPT in 2026? covers the on-page and technical tactics that make your own content easier for an AI tool to lift. Category authority is what gets you invited into the conversation in the first place.

Think of the three as layered, not competing. GEO tactics tune what an AI tool does with your page once it finds it. Category authority decides how often it finds you at all. Measuring visibility tells you whether either layer is actually working. Skip any one and the other two underperform.

What does a 90-day category authority checklist look like?

Sequencing matters more than intensity. Here's a realistic order for a UK SME with one marketing person and no dedicated PR budget, built around the platform types above rather than around any single AI tool.

Days 1 to 30: audit and claim

  • List every comparison site, review platform and directory relevant to your category, then check which listings already exist for you.
  • Claim and complete every listing you find, filling every optional field, not just the mandatory ones.
  • Add or correct Organisation and Product schema markup on your own site.
  • Check whether your category already has a Wikipedia or Wikidata entry, and note what's missing or wrong.

Days 31 to 60: build the habit

  • Set up a standing process for requesting reviews after every completed job or sale, not a one-off email blast.
  • Identify the one or two trade bodies most relevant to your category and start the membership application.
  • Check your Wikipedia and Wikidata presence, or your category's page, for factual errors and correct them with sources.
  • Draft one factual, UK-specific case study or statistic you can pitch to trade press later.

Days 61 to 90: earn independent coverage

  • Pitch a specific, factual story (a UK data point, a named case study) to trade press in your sector.
  • Follow up on the trade body application and prepare the member profile content it will ask for.
  • Re-check every listing from days 1 to 30 for accuracy, since comparison sites update pricing and features constantly.
  • Review which of the five platform types above still has no presence at all, and start it.

How do you know the category authority play is working?

You'll feel it before you can prove it. Prospects start referencing something you didn't tell them, using language that came from a comparison listing or a review rather than your own marketing. That's the early signal worth acting on, well before any dashboard confirms it.
