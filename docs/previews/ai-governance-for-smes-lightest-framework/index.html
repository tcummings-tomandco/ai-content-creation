What's the lightest AI governance framework for a UK SME?

There's no single mandatory UK AI governance framework in 2026. The government shelved its one-size-fits-all self-assessment tool, AI Management Essentials, in December 2025 after SMEs said it didn't fit their size. The practical minimum that satisfies the ICO is a named accountability owner, a risk register, a DPIA where personal data's involved, and a way to tell staff what's changed. ISO/IEC 42001 certification is optional, not required.

Why isn't there one official UK government AI governance framework yet?

The government actually tried to build one. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology ran a consultation on a self-assessment tool called AI Management Essentials (AIME) from 6 November 2024 to 29 January 2025, drawing 65 responses to a questionnaire built around three themes: internal processes, managing risks, and communication (gov.uk).

In its December 2025 response, DSIT said it wouldn't publish AIME after all, and wouldn't make it a government procurement requirement either (gov.uk). The idea wasn't the problem. Treating every business the same, a five-person start-up and a listed bank filling in the same questionnaire, didn't survive contact with the consultation responses.

Picture the two organisations sitting side by side. A five-person marketing consultancy using an off-the-shelf AI writing tool has almost nothing in common, risk-wise, with a 200-person manufacturer feeding sensor data into a predictive-maintenance model. AIME asked both the same questions. Respondents said that made it too shallow for the manufacturer and too heavy for the consultancy.

DSIT says it's now refining the approach into guidance focused purely on the foundational governance measures an SME actually needs, rather than one generic checklist for everyone. Until that lands, the "lightest framework" question has to be answered by combining what already exists, which is what the rest of this piece sets out to do.

AIME's three themes are still a useful shape even though the tool itself won't ship in its original form. Internal processes, managing risks and communication map neatly onto the ICO's own expectations below, so nothing from the consultation gets wasted. It's the delivery mechanism that changed, not the underlying logic.

What does the ICO actually expect from a business using AI?

The ICO isn't waiting for AI-specific law before it starts enforcing accountability. Its existing UK GDPR accountability framework already applies to AI systems, and its dedicated toolkit spells out what "accountable" looks like in practice (ico.org.uk).

Senior management can't hand this off to whoever built the model. The ICO's guidance is explicit that responsibility for understanding and addressing AI risk sits with a data protection officer, or a nominated data protection lead, with day-to-day oversight given to a steering group, committee or equivalent, not to individual engineers.

Take a 15-person recruitment agency trialling an AI CV-screening tool. Under the ICO's framework, "we bought a tool that does this" isn't an answer to who's accountable. Someone named has to be able to explain, on request, why the tool was chosen, what data feeds it, and who checks its output.

Every AI system that touches personal data needs its own line in the risk register, linked to the department that owns it, and a DPIA before anything likely high-risk goes live. Which framework should a UK SME actually use?

Three options sit on the table right now, and they're not mutually exclusive. Here's how they compare for a typical UK SME.

For most SMEs the answer is the first two together. The ICO's accountability basics are already a legal requirement the moment personal data's in play, and DSIT's introductory guidance gives you a structure without paying an external auditor (gov.uk).

Nothing here gets wasted if certification becomes necessary later, either. DSIT's own guidance for AIME confirmed the government built its thinking on the same foundations ISO/IEC 42001 uses, alongside the EU AI Act and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

ISO 42001 earns its cost the day a contract or tender specifically asks for it, not before. A software vendor selling into the public sector or a large enterprise customer is the classic case: the buyer's procurement team wants a certificate on file, not just a policy document, and no amount of internal rigour substitutes for the external audit they're asking for.

Do any UK sectors face extra AI governance rules on top of this?

Three sectors get an extra layer, and it's worth checking whether your business sits in one of them before you finalise a governance plan.

Financial services firms

The FCA hasn't written AI-specific rules. Instead it expects firms to fit AI governance inside the Senior Managers and Certification Regime and the Consumer Duty, the same accountability structures that already cover everything else a firm does (fca.org.uk). A named senior manager has to be able to show they took reasonable steps to control how AI is used, not just that a policy document exists.

In practice that means an AI-driven pricing model or a robo-advice tool needs the same outcome testing and fair-treatment monitoring the FCA already expects of a human underwriter. The tool changes. The accountability chain doesn't.

The FCA is also running the Mills Review into the long-term impact of AI on retail financial services (fca.org.uk). Today's principles-based approach is a starting point, not a permanent settlement, so a firm that builds proper accountability now has less to redo if the FCA firms up more specific AI rules later.

Legal services firms

The Law Society's guidance recommends a named senior individual for AI oversight, a committee that includes technical staff, and regular audits feeding the same risk register the ICO expects. According to SRA data it cites, around 75% of the UK's largest law firms use AI, against roughly 30% of small firms still only exploring it (lawsociety.org.uk).

Smaller firms have more governance catching up to do, not less. They're further behind on adoption, which means most of them haven't had to build the muscle yet.

Health-adjacent businesses

If an AI tool performs or supports a diagnostic or treatment function, the MHRA can classify it as Software as a Medical Device, which brings a separate registration and risk-classification regime on top of everything above (gov.uk). Admin and scheduling tools in a healthcare setting generally don't trigger this. Anything that reads a scan, triages a symptom or flags a diagnosis usually does, and the MHRA is actively reworking how that classification is graded by risk.

How many UK SMEs are already using AI without a governance framework?

More than half of UK firms, 54%, were actively using AI by March 2026, according to the British Chambers of Commerce's survey with Atos, up from 35% in 2025, 25% in 2024 and 23% in 2023 (britishchambers.org.uk). Around 94% of the firms surveyed were SMEs, so this is squarely a small-business story, not a corporate one.

Firms already using AI expect a net productivity gain of +71 percentage points over the next year, the same BCC survey found. That's a lot of businesses betting on a tool that, in most cases, nobody has formally written down who's responsible for.

Here's the part that should worry a board more than the checklist does. Adoption is moving faster than governance. A business that started using an AI tool eighteen months ago, well before any of this guidance existed, probably still hasn't gone back to write down who actually owns the risk.

That gap is exactly what the funding is now chasing. Direct UK government AI funding announced across the twelve months covered by the AI Opportunities Action Plan came to roughly £2.6 billion, including the smaller pots specifically aimed at assurance and adoption support (Tom & Co analysis of the AI Opportunities Action Plan: One Year On).

What free government support is coming for SME AI governance?

DSIT is building an AI Assurance Platform as a one-stop shop for assurance tools, services and frameworks, and it will host an AI Essentials Toolkit built specifically for start-ups and SMEs rather than large enterprises (gov.uk). Within that same funding picture, the AI Assurance Innovation Fund opened for applications in spring 2026 with £11 million to support new assurance mechanisms.

None of this is live as a finished SME product yet. But it's the clearest signal so far of where the "lightest framework" is heading: free, government-backed, and built for a business that's never run a formal management-system audit before, rather than adapted down from one that was designed for enterprise.

Until the toolkit lands, the most useful thing a UK SME can actually read today is DSIT's own "Introduction to AI assurance" document, which explains the underlying concepts the future toolkit will be built on without asking for an audit fee first.

Worth checking back in on this in six months rather than treating it as settled. A department that shelved one tool in December 2025 because it didn't fit SMEs is, by its own account, actively building the replacement, and a genuinely SME-shaped free option is the single biggest change that could simplify this whole picture.

None of that is a reason to wait. The ICO's accountability requirements are already in force today, regardless of what DSIT ships next, and they don't pause for a future toolkit.

What smaller firms without a compliance team can realistically do this month

Not every SME has a DPO on staff. If you don't, the nominated data protection lead can be a director wearing a second hat, provided they actually have the authority to say no to a project, not just the job title. That's the bar the ICO's guidance sets, and it's achievable without a new hire.

What should a UK SME's AI governance checklist look like this quarter?

  1. Name an owner. One senior person, ideally your DPO or a nominated data protection lead, accountable for AI risk. Not a rotating cast of whoever happened to buy the tool.
  2. Add AI to the risk register. Every system that touches personal data or a customer-facing decision gets its own line, linked to the department that runs it, reviewed at the same cadence as the rest of the register.
  3. Run a DPIA before anything customer-facing goes live. Do it before launch, not after a complaint forces the question, and keep the assessment on file for when a regulator or a customer asks to see it.
  4. Tell staff what's changed. A one-page policy beats a fifty-page one nobody reads: which tools are approved, what data can go into them, and who to ask when it's unclear.
  5. Leave ISO 42001 until a contract asks for it. Don't pre-empt a certification spend no customer has actually required yet, and don't let a vendor's sales pitch convince you otherwise.

Pick whichever of these five is missing right now and fix that one this month. The ICO doesn't expect a perfect AI management system on day one. It expects evidence that somebody in the business is actually responsible for the one you've got.
