How are UK financial services firms actually using AI in 2026?

Three in four UK financial services firms, 75%, were already using AI by November 2024, according to the Bank of England and FCA's third joint AI survey, with a further 10% planning to adopt it within three years. That's up from 58% in 2022. Most of that AI still sits in fraud detection, credit scoring and back-office automation, not customer-facing chatbots.

What share of UK financial services firms use AI in 2026?

The 75% figure comes from a joint survey the Bank of England and FCA ran across 118 firms in mid-2024, spanning six parts of the sector: UK banks, international banks, insurers, non-bank lenders, investment and capital markets firms, and financial market infrastructure and payments providers.

That's a sharp jump from the 58% who said they used AI in the equivalent 2022 survey, and from the 14% who said they were merely planning to. Adoption grew by roughly 17 percentage points in two years, and most of that growth came from firms doing more with tools they'd already started using rather than brand new adopters arriving from nowhere.

Here's the bit that's easy to miss. A fourth edition of the survey is confirmed to re-run in 2026, but the results aren't published yet, so November 2024 remains the most recent official number both regulators and firms are actually working from as they plan next year's budgets.

The survey's six-sector split matters for anyone reading the headline number as a single blob. Insurers, non-bank lenders and payments firms sit in the same sample as the largest UK and international banks, so 75% describes a genuinely broad slice of the regulated sector, not just the household-name high street names.

What are UK financial firms actually using AI for?

Most of that AI isn't generative. Foundation models, the category that includes ChatGPT-style large language models, made up just 17% of AI use cases in the 2024 survey. The rest is still traditional machine learning: fraud detection, anti-money-laundering monitoring, credit scoring and demand forecasting, technology that's been in production for years.

"Foundation model" is regulator-speak for the big, general-purpose systems, GPT-4 and Claude among them, that get fine-tuned or wrapped for a specific job, rather than a narrow tool built from scratch to do one thing, like flagging an unusual transaction.

Anti-money-laundering and fraud detection remain the two most mature AI use cases across UK banking, the categories firms have been running models against for the longest. That's part of why cybersecurity, not the AI itself, tops the risk list further down this article: attackers already target these systems directly.

A third of all AI use cases run through a third-party vendor rather than being built in-house, a share the Bank of England and FCA both expect to keep rising as outsourcing gets cheaper and as models get harder to build from scratch without specialist teams.

That third-party share is exactly what UK model risk rules are designed to catch. The PRA's SS1/23 model risk principles apply across a firm's entire operations, not just credit and market risk models, covering everything from an AI-driven customer chatbot to a machine-learning tool bought in for HR screening.

Firms themselves flagged 12% of their foundation-model use cases and 16% of their third-party use cases as "high materiality": ones where a failure would genuinely hurt customers, markets, or the firm's own safety and soundness, not just cause an internal headache.

"High materiality" is the industry's word for stakes, not sophistication: could this specific use case cause real harm if it went wrong. It's a judgement call firms make about themselves, not an external audit.

Combine those two figures and only around 2% of all AI use cases in UK financial services are both built on a foundation model and rated high materiality by the firm running them (Tom & Co analysis of Bank of England and FCA survey data, September 2026).

That's the honest scale of the "LLMs are quietly running the financial system" worry. It's real, but it sits inside a small slice of a much bigger AI estate that's mostly older, better-understood, tightly supervised models doing unglamorous jobs like flagging a suspicious transaction before it clears.

Which regulators oversee AI in UK financial services?

No UK regulator has written an AI-specific rulebook for financial services, and none has said it plans to. Instead, four existing regimes stretch to cover AI depending on what a firm is doing with it, who it might affect, and how much money or data sits behind it.

Regulator Role Key mechanism for AI Latest activity
FCA Conduct regulator for authorised firms Senior Managers & Certification Regime: a named senior manager accountable for each AI use case Third AI/ML survey findings, Nov 2024
PRA (Bank of England) Prudential regulator for banks, building societies and insurers SS1/23 Model Risk Management principles, in force since 17 May 2024, explicitly covering AI and machine learning models Model risk roundtable on AI/ML held Nov 2025
Bank of England (financial stability) Systemic risk oversight across the sector Co-runs the AI/ML adoption survey and tracks third-party concentration risk Fourth joint survey confirmed for 2026
ICO UK data protection regulator UK GDPR applies wherever an AI system processes personal data, including most credit scoring and fraud tools General AI guidance, not finance-specific

The FCA's Senior Managers & Certification Regime is doing most of the practical work here. Firms are expected to name the individual senior manager accountable for each AI use case, the same mechanism that already covers every other material risk on their books.

The Consumer Duty adds a further layer wherever AI touches a retail customer. Firms have to evidence good outcomes across four areas: product governance, price and value, consumer understanding and consumer support, whatever tool actually produced the decision.

In practice, that means a collections chatbot that can't spot financial distress and keeps sending standard debt-collection messages fails the Consumer Duty test, even if the underlying model is technically accurate and cheap to run.

The ICO doesn't run a finance-specific AI programme, but UK GDPR already applies in full wherever an AI system processes personal data. That covers most of the credit scoring, fraud detection and customer-facing tools on this article's use-case list, regardless of which sector regulator also has a stake in the outcome.

What risks are UK financial firms most worried about?

Cybersecurity, third-party dependency and model complexity dominate the risk conversation in the Bank of England and FCA's survey, and firms rank them differently depending on whether they're talking about today or the next three years.

Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is the single highest perceived systemic risk from AI, both now and looking three years out, according to the survey. AI expands the attack surface (new models, new data pipelines, new vendors) faster than most security teams can map it, let alone patch it.

Third-party dependency

Third-party dependency is the risk respondents expect to grow fastest over the next three years. With a third of AI use cases already outsourced, and that share rising, a single vendor outage or breach can now ripple across several unrelated firms at once.

That's also where the survey's own numbers point most sharply: third-party dependency was flagged as the single factor causing the largest increase in systemic risk of anything measured, ahead of both model complexity and embedded models on their own.

Model complexity and hidden models

Only 34% of firms say they have "complete understanding" of the AI they use; 46% say their understanding is only partial. That gap matters most for "hidden" models: AI quietly embedded inside a platform a firm didn't consciously choose to deploy.

A credit-decisioning tool bought as a simple rules engine back in 2019 may well be running a machine-learning model today, after a routine vendor update nobody in compliance was asked to sign off. Nobody set out to build a hidden model. It just happened.

Consumer Duty raises the stakes on hidden models specifically. A firm has to be able to explain, in terms a customer can actually understand, why an AI-driven decision was reached, which is a hard thing to do for a model nobody inside the building fully understands either.

Smaller non-bank lenders and payments firms are often the most exposed here. They're the most likely to buy AI wholesale from a single fintech vendor rather than build anything in-house, which concentrates both the efficiency gain and the third-party risk in the same relationship.

What did the FCA's AI Sprint conclude about the next five years?

The FCA's AI Sprint, held over two days in January 2025, brought together 115 participants from industry, academia, regulators and consumer groups to think through where AI in financial services is heading.

Two threads ran through the findings. More personalisation for consumers, as agentic AI (autonomous models that can act, not just answer) scales up how firms tailor products and interactions. And more automation inside firms themselves, particularly in compliance monitoring and fraud detection.

Participants also flagged risks the current rulebook wasn't built with in mind, including algorithmic collusion between firms running similar pricing models and a general lack of transparency in some AI-driven decisions, though most agreed existing principles-based regulation already stretches to cover the ground.

The consistent message from the room was that trust, not capability, is the bottleneck. Firms using the FCA's own sandboxes and data access routes to build that trust early tend to reach market faster than firms waiting for a settled rulebook that isn't coming.

How can a firm test AI with the regulator before it goes live?

The FCA runs three linked routes for firms that want regulatory input before they ship an AI product, not after a complaint or an enforcement letter arrives. Each one suits a different stage of building.

Route What it does Scale Latest cohort
AI Sprint Two-day cross-industry workshop on AI opportunities and risks 115 participants from industry, academia, regulators and consumer groups 29 to 30 January 2025
advanced AI sandbox programme Five-month cohort programme with GPU infrastructure, datasets and expert support to build and test AI use cases 23 firms selected from 132 applications in the first cohort Second cohort showcase 26 Nov 2026, built with Anthropic's Claude tools
AI Live Testing Regulatory support to test a near-finished AI system in real-world conditions 8 firms in the second cohort, including Barclays, Experian, Lloyds Banking Group and UBS Applications closed 24 Mar 2026; testing runs to end of 2026

Any FCA-authorised firm, or one working towards authorisation, can apply for AI Live Testing or the advanced AI sandbox programme. Neither route requires you to already be a large bank, which is exactly why fintechs like GoCardless and Palindrome sit in the same cohort as Barclays and UBS.

The Bank of England and FCA also co-chair an Artificial Intelligence Consortium, a standing group of AI experts from industry and academia established on 2 May 2025 that meets quarterly to feed real deployment experience back into how both regulators think about AI.

None of these routes are compulsory, and none replace a firm's own governance. What they buy is a documented conversation with the regulator before launch, which tends to go rather better than the same conversation after something's gone wrong.

What should a UK financial services firm do in the next 90 days?

None of this waits for new legislation. Every action below uses a mechanism that already exists in UK financial services regulation today, which is the point both the FCA and PRA keep making: the framework is already there, most firms just haven't mapped it onto their AI estate yet.

  1. Name the accountable senior manager for every live AI use case. SM&CR already requires this in principle; most firms haven't actually mapped it use case by use case.
  2. Classify each model against SS1/23, even if you're not a bank. Its five principles, covering identification, governance, development, validation and mitigants, are the cleanest available checklist for AI-specific model risk.
  3. Run outcome testing on any customer-facing AI under the Consumer Duty. Evidence that the AI is producing good results for real customers, not just that it clears an accuracy benchmark in a lab.
  4. Complete a DPIA before any AI system touches personal data. Our practical DPIA template for AI sets out what the ICO expects to see.
  5. Watch the FCA AI Lab application windows. The next advanced AI sandbox and AI Live Testing cohorts are among the cheapest ways to get direct regulatory feedback before a full rollout.
  6. Read the AI Consortium's quarterly minutes. They're the clearest public signal of where supervisory attention on AI is heading next, months before it turns into formal guidance.

None of these six actions need a bigger AI budget. They need someone in risk or compliance spending a week mapping what's already been bought against what SM&CR, SS1/23 and the Consumer Duty already ask for.
