What does a DPIA for AI need to include under UK GDPR?

A DPIA for an AI system follows the ICO's standard seven-step process (identify the need, describe the processing, consult, assess necessity, identify risks, mitigate, sign off), but each step needs AI-specific evidence: training data provenance, accuracy testing, and proof a human reviewer can genuinely override an automated output. Skip it and you risk a fine of up to £8.7 million or 2% of global turnover.

Do you legally need a DPIA for your AI system?

Yes, in most cases. UK GDPR Article 35 requires a DPIA for any processing "likely to result in a high risk" to people's rights and freedoms. The ICO's own published list adds ten further triggers to the law's three statutory ones, and innovative technology, explicitly including AI and machine learning, is one of them.

Take a UK recruitment agency piloting an AI shortlisting tool this year. It profiles candidates by scoring them, applies a novel use of existing technology, and feeds a decision about who gets an interview. That is at least two of the nine risk criteria the ICO's own screening guidance borrows from the European DPIA criteria, and meeting two is the ICO's own rule of thumb for needing one.

29% of UK businesses reported using at least one AI technology in June 2026, up 8 percentage points on a year earlier, according to the ONS Business Insights and Conditions Survey. A good number of them should be asking the innovative-technology question above and are not.

If your AI system profiles people, feeds a decision about them, or uses a model trained on personal data in a way you have not risk-assessed before, assume you need a DPIA and work backwards from there.

A DPIA is a UK GDPR requirement, not an AI-specific one, so it sits alongside whatever sector rules already apply. A financial services firm using AI in credit decisions still answers to the FCA on top of the ICO. A healthcare provider using AI diagnostics still answers to the MHRA. The DPIA shows your data protection homework was done before the sector regulator asks.

One question trips up nearly every business buying rather than building: who owns the DPIA, you or the vendor? Under UK GDPR, it is you. If you decide what the AI system does with people's data, you are the controller, and the obligation sits with you even when a supplier built the model.

A vendor's compliance documentation helps with Step 2 below, describing the processing. It is useful evidence, not a substitute for your own assessment of the risk to your customers or employees specifically.

What does the ICO's DPIA process actually involve?

The ICO does not run a separate process for AI. It uses the same seven-step DPIA framework it has run since 2018, just with sharper questions at each step once a model, rather than a spreadsheet, is doing the deciding.

Step 1: Identify the need for a DPIA

Document why you are doing a DPIA at all: what the AI system does, why now, and which of the ICO's high-risk triggers apply. For most AI projects this is a one-page note, not a debate. If you are unsure, the safer, cheaper option is nearly always to do the DPIA.

Step 2: Describe the processing

Set out what data goes in, how the model uses it, what comes out, and who is affected. For AI specifically, that means naming the training data source, whether it includes personal data, and whether the vendor retrains the model on your inputs. Vague answers here undermine every later step.

Pull this straight from your vendor contract rather than guessing. A supplier that cannot tell you where the training data came from, or whether your inputs feed back into the model, has just handed you your first unmitigated risk before the DPIA has properly started.

Step 3: Consider consultation

Decide who you need to hear from before you go ahead: employees affected by a workforce-monitoring tool, customers affected by a pricing algorithm, or your own DPO. The ICO's own worked example runs user testing and a feedback questionnaire alongside internal sign-off, not instead of it.

Step 4: Assess necessity and proportionality

Explain why the AI system is necessary for your stated purpose and no more invasive than the job requires. If a simpler rules-based tool would do the same job with less personal data, the ICO expects you to have considered it and said why you did not choose it.

Step 5: Identify and assess the risks

Score the likelihood and severity of harm to individuals, not to your business. Bias in a training dataset, an opaque decision a person cannot challenge, and model drift after deployment are the three risks that come up in almost every AI DPIA worth the name.

Step 6: Identify measures to mitigate the risks

For each risk, name the specific control: bias testing before launch, a documented human review step with real authority to overrule the system, or a retraining schedule with a named owner. "We will monitor it" is not a mitigation on its own.

Set a cadence for each control too. Quarterly bias re-testing catches drift a one-off launch check never will, and it gives you dated evidence if the ICO ever asks what you actually did rather than what you planned to do.

Step 7: Sign off, record and integrate outcomes

Get sign-off from someone with real authority to stop the project, usually your DPO or a board-level owner, record their advice in writing, and if you decide not to follow it, write down why. Then feed the actions into your actual project plan, not a filing cabinet.

What should an AI-specific DPIA template include, section by section?

You do not need to build a template from scratch. The ICO publishes its own sample DPIA template free to adapt. The table below shows where a generic version falls short for AI projects, and what to add.

The generic template was built for processing you can fully explain: a new CRM field, a marketing list, a CCTV install. AI breaks that assumption. Nobody, including the vendor, can always tell you exactly why a model produced one output rather than another. The AI-specific additions below exist to make that opacity visible on paper rather than quietly ignored.

DPIA sectionStandard DPIA questionAI-specific addition
Describe the processingWhat data goes in and out?Name the model, version, training data source, and whether the vendor retrains on your inputs.
Necessity and proportionalityIs this the least invasive option?Record whether a non-AI or simpler rules-based alternative was considered and rejected, and why.
Risk identificationWhat could go wrong for the individual?Score bias, explainability and model drift as named risk categories, not just security and access risks.
MitigationsWhat controls reduce the risk?Document bias testing results, the human review process, and who owns model retraining.
Sign-offWho approved this and on what basis?Name the person who can genuinely stop deployment, not just note that the DPIA was "reviewed".
ReviewWhen will this be revisited?Set a review date tied to model retraining or vendor updates, not just an annual calendar date.

Every row above should point to evidence: a document, a test result, a named person, not a description of good intentions. That is the difference between a DPIA that survives an ICO inquiry and one that does not.

How does prior consultation with the ICO work if risk remains high?

If your DPIA still shows a high residual risk after every mitigation, UK GDPR requires you to consult the ICO before you go ahead, not after. You cannot deploy the system in the meantime.

The ICO aims to respond within eight weeks of receiving your DPIA. In complex cases, it can extend that by six weeks, taking the maximum wait to 14 weeks.

Tom & Co analysis of the ICO's own published response windows: the worst-case wait for a prior-consultation reply is 75% longer than the standard one, 98 days against 56, once the maximum extension applies.

Build that runway into your launch plan now. An AI system that needs prior consultation and is scheduled to go live in six weeks is already behind before a single line of code changes.

The better outcome is never reaching this step at all. A DPIA that genuinely reduces residual risk, real human override, tested mitigations, a named owner, usually gets you below the "high risk" bar the law sets for mandatory consultation. Treat the 14-week wait as the cost of a thin DPIA, not a fixed tax on using AI.

What happens if a business skips the DPIA or gets it wrong?

The ICO fined MediaLab.AI, Inc., owner of the image-sharing platform Imgur, £247,590 on 4 February 2026. Among the failures the ICO found: no DPIA had been carried out to assess and mitigate the risks to children whose data the platform processed between 2021 and 2025.

That case was not about an AI model specifically. It is still the clearest recent example of what the ICO actually checks for: not whether you meant well, but whether a DPIA exists and was acted on.

Failing to carry out a DPIA when the law requires one sits in the ICO's lower fine tier, up to £8.7 million or 2% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. That is before you count the cost of pausing or unwinding a live AI system the ICO orders you to stop using.

What the ICO actually asks for in an investigation is rarely a philosophical defence of your AI strategy. It is the paper trail: the dated DPIA, the named sign-off, the test results behind your mitigations. A business that can produce all three in an afternoon is in a completely different position to one that has to reconstruct them from memory once the complaint has already landed.

What should a UK DPO or legal lead do with this template this quarter?

  1. Screen every live AI system against the ICO's high-risk triggers this month, starting with anything that profiles people or feeds an automated decision.
  2. Adapt the ICO's own DPIA template using the AI-specific additions in the table above, rather than reusing a generic version built for a different kind of project.
  3. Name a real human reviewer for every automated decision, someone who can explain, on request, why they overrode the system, not just someone who signs it off.
  4. Build the 14-week worst case into your launch timeline for any system that might need prior consultation with the ICO.
  5. Set a review date tied to your next model update or vendor contract renewal, not a generic annual box-tick.

None of these five actions need a big budget or an outside consultant to start. A DPO with an afternoon, the ICO's own template, and the table in this article can screen and document the first system today.

Pick one AI system already live in your business without a documented DPIA, and run it through the seven steps above this week. That is the gap the ICO's enforcement record shows businesses leave unclosed most often.
