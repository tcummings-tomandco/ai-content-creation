What does the ICO's guidance mean for AI and UK GDPR in 2026?

UK GDPR fully applies to AI systems: the ICO's existing guidance on lawfulness, fairness, transparency and accountability governs how you build and use them, and since 5 February 2026 the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 has replaced Article 22 with a more permissive but safeguard-heavy regime for automated decisions. Get it wrong and the ICO can fine you up to £17.5 million or 4% of global turnover.

What changed in UK GDPR for AI on 5 February 2026?

For most of the last two decades, UK data protection law took a hard line on solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects on someone. Article 22 of the UK GDPR made them a general prohibition, with narrow exceptions. That changed with the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, which received royal assent on 19 June 2025.

Section 80 of the Act came into force on 5 February 2026, replacing Article 22 with a permissive, safeguard-led regime. Organisations can now rely on any lawful basis for a significant automated decision, not just consent or contract, provided the right safeguards sit behind it.

Tom & Co analysis of the two published statutory dates: businesses had 231 days between the Act receiving royal assent and Section 80 actually taking effect, the real runway UK organisations had to update their automated decision-making before the new rules bit.

The safeguards are the trade-off. Anyone significantly affected by a solely automated decision must be told about it, be able to make representations, ask for a human to intervene, and contest the outcome. Special category data, health, ethnicity, biometric data used to identify someone, keeps a much tighter restriction than everything else.

In practice that means a written route: how to ask for a human review, a realistic timeframe for a reply, and someone named who can actually deliver it. A policy that just says "contact us" without a process behind it won't survive an ICO complaint.

Picture a UK recruitment agency that's used AI shortlisting software since 2023. Under the old Article 22 regime, relying solely on the tool's score to reject a candidate sat in a legal grey area unless consent or contract covered it. Under Section 80, the agency can rely on legitimate interests instead, provided a real person can review a challenged decision and actually change it.

Here's the bit lawyers keep tripping over. "Permissive" doesn't mean unregulated. The general ban is gone, but the ICO has replaced it with a longer list of things you have to prove you did, not fewer.

What does the ICO's AI and data protection guidance actually require?

The ICO hasn't waited for Parliament to catch up. It already runs a stack of AI-specific guidance sitting alongside the core UK GDPR text, most of it built around four themes: lawfulness, fairness, transparency and accountability.

None of this guidance is itself statutory. But tribunals and the ICO's own enforcement priorities lean on it heavily, so treating it as optional because it isn't primary legislation is a mistake most DPOs only make once. None of it replaces the underlying UK GDPR principles, it just spells out what they mean in an AI context specifically.

Guidance on AI and data protection

This is the anchor document. First published in 2020 and updated on 15 March 2023 after industry asked for clearer fairness expectations, it sets out how accountability, lawfulness, fairness, transparency, DPIAs and bias mitigation apply specifically to AI, not to general data processing.

AI and data protection risk toolkit

A practical companion, not just prose. Version 1.0 landed in 2022 after alpha and beta testing through 2021, built as a working checklist a DPO can run an AI project against rather than a policy document to file away.

Generative AI consultation series

A five-part consultation ran from January to September 2024, covering the lawful basis for scraping training data, purpose limitation, accuracy, individual rights and controller status across the AI supply chain.

The ICO published its outcomes report on 12 December 2024, and its headline position hasn't moved since: legitimate interests is the only lawful basis the ICO accepts for scraping personal data to train a generative AI model. When does a decision count as "solely automated" under the new rules?

This is where most of the compliance risk actually sits. Get the classification wrong and you either apply safeguards you didn't need, or, worse, skip them when you should have applied them.

The ICO's position, carried through into its 2026 draft ADM guidance, is blunt: human involvement has to be active and genuine. A person needs the authority, competence and information to actually change the outcome, not just to sign off on whatever the system recommends.

"Token gesture" is the ICO's own phrase, and it's doing a lot of work. If your human reviewer couldn't explain, on the spot, why they'd override the system, or never has, that review probably isn't taking your process outside the ADM rules at all.

A significant decision, under the new definition, is one with a legal effect or a similarly significant effect on the person: recruitment shortlisting, credit decisions, insurance pricing, benefits eligibility. Special category personal data keeps the tighter restriction regardless of how permissive the general regime has become.

Take a UK lender running AI credit scoring. If the underwriting team can override a rejection but never actually does, and can't point to a case where they did, the ICO's guidance treats that as solely automated in substance, whatever the process diagram says.

This is also where the DPIA earns its keep. A DPIA that actually documents who reviews what, and how often they override the system, is the evidence an ICO investigation will ask for first.

What happens when a business gets AI and UK GDPR wrong?

The ICO can fine an organisation up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, for the most serious breaches. A lower tier caps out at £8.7 million or 2% for failings such as poor record-keeping, per the ICO's own fining guidance.

Run the maths for a mid-size UK business on roughly £5 million turnover: the 4% cap alone works out at £200,000, well before the case gets anywhere near the £17.5 million fixed ceiling. Turnover, not headcount, is what decides which number actually applies.

Clearview AI: fined £7,552,800 in May 2022 for scraping UK residents' facial images without a lawful basis. The First-tier Tribunal overturned it in October 2023 on jurisdiction grounds. The Upper Tribunal allowed the ICO's appeal on 6 October 2025, ruling that UK data protection law does reach any company monitoring UK residents' behaviour regardless of where it's based, and sent the case back for the First-tier Tribunal to reconsider.

The case isn't resolved. But the direction of travel is clear: extraterritorial reach for AI companies scraping or processing UK residents' data is getting harder to escape on a jurisdiction technicality, not easier, per the ICO's own account of the Upper Tribunal judgment.

Three years and two tribunal rulings after the original fine, the case is still not finally settled, a fair warning about how long AI enforcement disputes can run.

The ICO is also engaging directly with the AI supply chain rather than waiting for complaints to land. It's currently working with 11 major foundation model developers, gathering evidence on their data protection compliance and pushing for concrete assurances on how they mitigate harm.

The ICO's AI and biometrics strategy singles out recruitment ADM, police facial recognition and foundation models as its highest-priority areas for 2026. If your AI system touches any of the three, expect closer scrutiny than the average data processing complaint gets.

What does a UK GDPR AI compliance checklist look like for 2026?

  1. Run or refresh a DPIA for every AI system that processes personal data, not just the ones that feel obviously risky.
  2. Document your lawful basis under the new Section 80 options, and check whether any decision touches special category data.
  3. Build genuine human review into every solely automated significant decision, and be ready to show it's real, not decorative.
  4. Check your generative AI training data against legitimate interests, the ICO's only accepted lawful basis for scraping personal data.
  5. Size your risk register against the higher fine tier (£17.5 million or 4% of turnover), not the lower one.

None of these are one-off tasks. The ICO's final ADM guidance is due in summer 2026, with a statutory ADM code following in 2027, so this checklist is a starting position, not a box to tick once and forget.

Most UK organisations already have three or four of these five partly done. The gap is usually the same one: a lawful basis and a human review process that exist on paper but were never tested against a real, contested decision.

What should a UK DPO or legal lead do this quarter?

Start with the DPIA backlog. Most UK organisations using AI in any operational process, recruitment, marketing personalisation, fraud checks, already have a gap between what they're doing and what's actually on file.

Then test every "human in the loop" claim in your own processes against the ICO's active-and-genuine standard. If nobody can explain why a reviewer overrode a system's recommendation last month, that's the first thing to fix.

This isn't purely a legal problem either. Whoever builds or buys the AI system needs the same checklist on their desk, not just the DPO's. Ignoring this quarter's checklist doesn't remove the risk, it just moves the first conversation about it from your desk to the ICO's.
