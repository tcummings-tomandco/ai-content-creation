RAG vs fine-tuning vs prompt engineering: which do you need?

Use prompt engineering first, it's free and instant. Reach for RAG when the right answer depends on information the model was never trained on, your prices, your policies, this week's regulation. Fine-tune only when you need consistent behaviour a good prompt can't pin down, and check first: OpenAI stopped taking new fine-tuning customers on its main platform on 7 May 2026, and peer-reviewed research from EMNLP 2024 found RAG beats unsupervised fine-tuning on both old and new knowledge.

What's actually different between RAG, fine-tuning, and prompt engineering?

The three get lumped together constantly, and teams waste weeks picking the wrong one. They are not competing options so much as three different levers. Prompting changes how you ask. RAG changes what the model can see. Fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. Here's each one in plain terms.

A UK example makes the difference concrete. A conveyancing firm asking a model to summarise a completion statement in plain English is a prompting problem. The same firm asking it to answer questions about the specific mortgage offer in its case management system is a RAG problem.

Teaching that model to always flag a named list of red-flag clauses, in the firm's own risk language, every single time, is closer to a fine-tuning problem, assuming prompting and examples genuinely can't hold it (they often can).

Prompt engineering

Writing and structuring your instructions so the model does what you want, first time. No infrastructure, no training run, no cost beyond the tokens you already pay for. It's the cheapest lever by a wide margin, and the one to exhaust before reaching for anything else.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)

The model retrieves relevant text from a source you control (your documents, a database, a policy library) before it answers, then writes its response grounded in that material. Our plain-English explainer on RAG covers the mechanics step by step.

Fine-tuning

Retraining a model's own weights on examples of the behaviour you want, so it does that thing reliably without being told each time. It's the most expensive and slowest lever, and, as this article covers below, one vendor is actively making it harder to buy.

Layering is normal. A production system might run a fine-tuned classifier, fed by a RAG pipeline, driven by a carefully written prompt, all three levers doing a different job in the same pipeline rather than competing for the same one.

How do you decide which one to use for a UK project?

Work through it as a short branching sequence rather than a preference. Each node has a clean yes or no, and most projects resolve in one or two steps rather than reaching the end.

Answer the questions in order and stop at the first "yes" that applies. Skipping ahead to fine-tuning because it sounds more sophisticated is the single most common way UK teams overspend on an AI build.

  1. Does the correct answer depend on information the model doesn't already know (your prices, your contracts, a regulation that changed this month)? If yes, go to RAG. If no, go to step 2.
  2. Do you need the model to behave in a strict, repeatable way that good prompting and examples can't achieve, a fixed output format, a house style, a narrow classification task? If yes, go to step 3. If no, stay with prompt engineering.
  3. Is that behaviour worth the cost, lock-in, and reduced vendor choice that fine-tuning now carries? If yes, fine-tune. If no, layer RAG and stronger prompting instead, most teams get further with that combination than they expect.

Plenty of production systems never reach step 3. They just prompt well, then add retrieval when the knowledge gap shows up.

Run the sequence on a real case: a mid-sized UK retailer wants a customer service assistant that answers questions about live stock and delivery dates. Step 1 is yes straight away, stock levels change hourly and no amount of clever prompting invents that data. The answer is RAG, pointed at the retailer's own inventory feed, not a fine-tuning project.

When does prompt engineering alone do the job?

More often than most technical teams assume. If the model already has the general knowledge it needs, and the task is really about format, tone, or reasoning steps, a well-written prompt with a couple of worked examples usually closes the gap.

Classification against a fixed, small set of categories. Rewriting text to a house style. Summarising a document that's pasted directly into the context window. Structured data extraction where you can show the model exactly what "done" looks like. None of these need retrieval or retraining.

Modern context windows help enormously here. When a model can hold tens or hundreds of thousands of words of context in a single prompt, "just paste the document in" covers far more ground than it did even a couple of years ago. Reach for a retrieval pipeline once that document set grows too large or too changeable to paste by hand, not before.

The tell that you've outgrown prompting isn't difficulty, it's information. The prompt keeps growing because you're pasting in more and more context by hand every time. That's the signal to automate retrieval instead of writing longer instructions.

A UK accountancy practice drafting client emails is a good example of prompting done properly rather than abandoned too early. Give the model the firm's tone guide, two or three real examples of a good email, and a short checklist of what "done" looks like.

Most of the quality gap closes without touching a vector database or a training run. The fence-sitters here (teams tempted to reach for RAG "just in case") usually find a tighter prompt does the job on its own.

When should you reach for RAG instead?

RAG earns its place the moment the useful answer lives outside the model's training data and inside something you control. A support system answering from this month's product manual. A legal assistant citing the actual clause in a contract. A compliance tool quoting the live version of a policy, not whatever the model half-remembers from training.

Cost is the other reason it wins by default. Retraining a model every time a price list or a policy document changes is expensive and slow. Re-indexing a folder of documents is neither. For any knowledge that updates monthly, weekly, or daily, that difference alone settles the question before accuracy even enters the conversation.

The audit trail matters as much as the accuracy. Because RAG can point to the exact passage behind a claim, it suits UK sectors where an unverifiable AI output is a liability, and the ICO's guidance on AI and data protection expects organisations to be able to explain automated outputs, not just produce them.

Unsupervised fine-tuning offers some improvement, but RAG consistently outperforms it, both for knowledge the model already saw in training and for entirely new knowledge, according to Ovadia et al., presented at EMNLP 2024.

That finding matters for the decision tree above. If you're weighing RAG against fine-tuning specifically to inject new or niche knowledge, the published research already leans hard one way.

The same paper's authors point to why: fine-tuning bakes facts into the model's weights, which makes it sensitive to how a question is phrased and prone to overfitting on whatever training examples it saw. RAG hands the model the actual passage at answer time, so a slightly different phrasing of the same question still finds the right document.

There's a UK adoption angle too. Per the DSIT AI Adoption Research, 85% of UK businesses already using AI have adopted text generation and natural language tools (the prompting-and-RAG end of the spectrum), against just 7% for agentic AI, the more complex, harder-to-govern end.

Scaled across the whole UK business population, that's roughly 13.6% of all UK firms using generative, prompt-driven AI against about 1.1% running agentic systems, a gap of around 12 times (Tom & Co analysis of DSIT adoption data). Most of the market solves problems with prompting and retrieval long before it needs anything heavier.

When is fine-tuning still worth it, and why is it getting harder to justify?

Fine-tuning still wins when you need durable, repeatable behaviour that no amount of prompting fixes: a strict internal classification scheme with hundreds of edge cases, a narrow task run millions of times where a smaller fine-tuned model beats a larger general one on cost, or house style so specific that examples alone won't hold.

The market for it just got smaller. OpenAI began winding down its self-serve fine-tuning platform from 7 May 2026: organisations that had never run a fine-tuning job could no longer start one from that date. The restriction widened on 2 July 2026 to cover accounts inactive for 60 days.

By 6 January 2027 even existing customers lose the ability to create new fine-tuning jobs entirely. Anthropic never offered a general-purpose fine-tuning API of its own either. The only route to a fine-tuned Claude model runs through Amazon Bedrock, currently limited to Claude 3 Haiku, not the current flagship models.

Where fine-tuning is still on offer, it isn't free to try. OpenAI's published pricing lists standard supervised fine-tuning for GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o at $25 per million training tokens, about £18.50 at September 2026 exchange rates (Tom & Co sterling conversion, approximate GBP/USD market rate).

The newer reinforcement fine-tuning route for o4-mini bills training compute at $100 an hour, roughly £74 an hour. Retrieval and prompting carry no equivalent training bill at all.

None of this rules fine-tuning out entirely. A UK insurer running the same narrow claims-triage classification millions of times a year, where a small fine-tuned model beats a larger general-purpose one on latency and per-call cost, still has a legitimate case. The point isn't that fine-tuning is dead. It's that the decision now needs a vendor-availability check that didn't exist eighteen months ago.

So the honest advice for most UK teams in 2026: treat fine-tuning as a last resort, not a default, and check vendor availability before you plan around it.

What should a UK team do about each path?

Match the three options against your actual constraint before committing engineering time.
