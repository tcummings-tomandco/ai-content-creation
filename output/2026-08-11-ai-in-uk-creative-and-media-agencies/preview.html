How are UK creative and media agencies using AI in 2026?

Just over half of UK creative industries businesses (51%, against a UK-wide average of 33%) already use AI in some form, according to the government's own 2026 AI Adoption Plan for the sector. The technology is reshaping ideation, production and client reporting fastest at agencies, while UK copyright law, tested for the first time in November 2025 in Getty Images v Stability AI, and Advertising Standards Authority rules both apply in full to AI-made work.

What is the current state of AI adoption in UK creative and media agencies?

That 51% headline hides a wide spread across the creative industries. Design and designer fashion businesses report 53% AI adoption, film, TV, video and photography 44%, and music and performing arts trail at 22%, according to the AI Adoption Plan for the Creative Industries, published by the government's AI Champion for the sector in June 2026.

Zoom out to the whole economy and the pattern holds. The Office for National Statistics found 58% of Information and Communication sector businesses used at least one AI technology by June 2026, the highest of any UK sector, against a national average that rose from around 12% in late 2023 to roughly 35% by mid-2026.

The sector doing the adopting is not small. UK creative industries businesses employed about 2.4 million people, roughly 7% of all UK jobs, in the year to June 2024, and generated £124 billion in gross value added in 2023, around 5% of total UK economic output, per DCMS's own Economic Estimates.

Tom & Co analysis: the creative-industries sub-sector with the highest AI adoption, IT, software and computer services at 60% (per the government's 2026 AI Champion review), is also the sector's single largest earner, contributing £49.1 billion of the creative industries' £124 billion gross value added in 2023, nearly 40% of the total (DCMS Economic Estimates, published December 2024). The adoption figure and the GVA figure are from different years, so treat this as a structural snapshot of where AI is concentrated by value, not a claim that AI itself drove the growth.

None of this is abstract for an agency. (If half your sector is already using the tools your clients are asking about, "should we use AI" stopped being the live question some time ago.) The live question is which use cases actually pay off, and which ones are landmines.

What AI use cases are actually paying back for agencies right now?

UK advertisers have moved fast. The proportion running at least one live generative AI use case rose from 9% in April 2024 to 41% by July 2025, according to ISBA's advertiser survey, reported by Marketing Week. Another 27% are experimenting and 28% are still exploring, so the market has not settled.

Efficiency, not creative effectiveness, is the reason. 62% of advertisers surveyed said saving time and money was the main point of their generative AI use, not making better ads. That's worth sitting with: the tool is being bought for the invoice line, not the idea.

Concepting and ideation

Agencies use generative tools to multiply first-round concepts fast: dozens of taglines, mood boards or storyboard frames in the time a single round used to take. The value isn't the AI-made version, which rarely survives contact with a client. It's more raw material to react to, earlier.

Production and asset variants

Resizing a hero campaign into forty social formats, translating copy, or drafting product photography variants are jobs generative AI now does at a fraction of the previous cost. This is the "efficiency, not effectiveness" 62% talking, not a creative leap.

Client reporting and measurement

Pulling campaign data into a client-ready report used to eat a day a month per account. AI-assisted reporting tools now draft the narrative around the numbers, leaving an account lead to check it and add judgement rather than build it from scratch.

Is AI actually costing jobs at UK agencies?

Here the data gets uncomfortable. IPA member agencies employed 24,963 people on 1 September 2025, down 6.8% from 26,787 a year earlier, according to the IPA Agency Census 2025. The fall was sharpest at creative and other non-media agencies, where employment dropped 14.3%; media agency employment actually grew 2.4% over the same year.

AI is one driver of that, not the only one. 8% of agencies say they cut headcount in the past 12 months directly because of AI, and 24% expect to in the next 12. Split by agency type, that expectation nearly triples for creative and non-media agencies (30%) against media agencies (10%).

88.3% of IPA member agencies say AI is having a considerable impact on how they work, so the headcount numbers aren't a fringe view. (Worth saying plainly: fewer people doing the same work is not automatically bad for a client, but it is exactly the conversation an agency's own staff deserve before it happens to them, not after.)

Which UK regulators and rules actually apply to agency AI use?

Four bodies matter most for a UK agency using AI day to day. None of them has written AI-specific rules from scratch. All four say their existing powers already reach it.

The IPO's copyright and AI consultation, which ran from December 2024 to February 2025, drew more than 11,000 responses, an unusually high number for a copyright policy consultation. In its statement of progress, published 15 December 2025, government set out three objectives for any reform: giving rights holders control over how their work trains AI models, giving AI developers workable access to training data, and building transparency about what training data was actually used.

For an agency that licenses stock imagery, commissions illustration or manages client IP, that access-versus-control tension isn't academic. It decides whether the AI tool an account team adopts this quarter is trained on properly licensed material or on material nobody signed off.

The Advertising Standards Authority takes a simpler line: there's no AI exemption in the Advertising Codes. An AI-generated image that would mislead, offend or cause harm breaks the rules exactly as a photographed one would, and the regulator has reported handling a growing number of AI-related complaints through 2025.

That reach is deliberately media-neutral. A chatbot-drafted product claim, an AI-voiced radio spot and a synthetic model in a fashion shoot all have to clear the same bar for accuracy and social responsibility a human-made version would. There's no separate, lighter-touch rulebook for "the AI did it".

What does the Getty Images v Stability AI ruling mean for agencies using AI tools?

The UK's first real test of this came on 4 November 2025, when the High Court handed down judgment in Getty Images v Stability AI ([2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch)). Getty's secondary copyright infringement claim, over Stability's use of millions of scraped Getty images to train Stable Diffusion, was dismissed.

Getty's trade mark claim fared little better. The court dismissed the claim under section 10(3) of the Trade Marks Act outright, and found only "extremely limited" infringement under sections 10(1) and 10(2), and only for early versions of the model.

Here's the bit agencies actually need. This wasn't a ruling that training on copyrighted work is lawful in general. Getty dropped its primary copyright claim before trial for reasons specific to its own case, so the court never decided the big question everyone wanted answered. What it decided, narrowly, is that Stability wasn't liable on the secondary claim actually put to it.

Treat that as an open question, not a green light. An agency briefing a generative tool for client work still needs to know what the vendor trained it on, because the case that eventually settles the point Getty's claim left open hasn't been decided yet.

The trade mark side of the judgment carries its own lesson. Even where the copyright claim failed outright, Stability still picked up "extremely limited" trade mark liability for its earlier model versions, the ones trained and shipped before the company tightened its process. An agency vetting a generative tool should ask what changed between the version a vendor trained two years ago and the one being pitched today, not just what the vendor's marketing page says about the current release.

What funding is available for AI adoption in the UK creative industries?

Government backed the sector with money as well as policy. The Creative Industries Sector Plan, published 23 June 2025, put £380 million behind a ten-year ambition to grow business investment in the sector from £17 billion to £31 billion by 2035.

£25 million of that goes to five new CoSTAR satellite labs and two demonstrator spaces, extending the network of Convergent Screen Technologies and performance in Realtime hubs that already work on AI-driven production tools. A further tranche doubles funding for the existing Creative Industries Clusters programme, which pairs universities with creative businesses on applied research including AI.

£200 million of the package is earmarked for regions outside London specifically, which matters for a sector the government's own figures show is heavily concentrated in the capital. An agency outside London chasing this money is chasing a stated policy priority, not a rounding error in a bigger London-focused pot.

The plan also commits to expanding support available through the British Business Bank for creative businesses looking to grow and hire, alongside the ten-year ambition to nearly double sector business investment to £31 billion by 2035. None of that money is earmarked for AI specifically in the way the CoSTAR and clusters funding is, but a creative business applying for growth finance now sits inside a policy environment that explicitly wants it to scale, AI-enabled or not.

What should a UK creative or media agency do about AI in the next 90 days?

  1. Audit what's already live. Most agencies underestimate how much generative AI is already in daily use once every team is asked directly, not just the tools that went through formal sign-off.
  2. Get a straight answer on training data provenance. Ask every generative vendor what it trained on and get it in writing, before the question Getty's case left open gets settled by a ruling that isn't as forgiving to AI vendors as this one was.
  3. Update client and supplier contracts. The IPA's own members are already doing this: 10% had revised contracts to cover generative AI use by September 2025, with a further 37% mid-revision, so a client asking about your paperwork is not a hypothetical.
  4. Decide the AI-disclosure line before a client asks. The ASA doesn't require disclosure, but a client blindsided by an AI-generated deliverable they didn't know about is a harder conversation than one had upfront.
  5. Have the headcount conversation on your own terms. If AI is going to change what a role looks like on an account, staff hearing that from their own agency beats hearing it after the fact, especially with 24% of agencies already expecting AI-driven cuts in the next year.
  6. Name an owner. Someone specific, not "the team", should be able to answer what a regulator or a nervous client asks about how AI is used on their account.
