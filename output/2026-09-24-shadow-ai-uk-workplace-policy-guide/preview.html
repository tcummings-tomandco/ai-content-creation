What is shadow AI and how should a UK employer manage it?

Shadow AI is staff using AI tools their employer hasn't approved or doesn't know about. Nearly a third of UK employees who use generative AI at work do so without their employer's knowledge, and workers now spend close to £958 million of their own money a year on tools for the job (Deloitte UK, September 2026). A written AI use policy, not a ban, is the fix that actually works.

What is shadow AI and how common is it in the UK?

Shadow AI isn't really new. It's the same pattern as shadow IT, staff signing up for Dropbox or Slack before the company did, just faster and harder to spot because a ChatGPT tab looks like any other browser tab.

Deloitte UK's inaugural GenAI Workforce Survey, the largest single-country study of workplace GenAI use to date, put a number on it. Fielded between 7 May and 10 June 2026 among 25,000 UK workers, it found 63% of UK workers knowingly use GenAI for work, and 31% of those users do so without their employer knowing. Almost one in ten has used a tool their employer bans outright.

Nearly a third of UK employees who use generative AI at work do so without telling their employer (Deloitte UK GenAI Workforce Survey, September 2026).

A separate Microsoft-commissioned Censuswide survey of 2,003 UK employees, fielded October 2025, found 71% had used an unapproved consumer AI tool at least once, and 51% kept doing so week on week. The most common uses: drafting or replying to work messages (49%), preparing reports and presentations (40%), and finance-related tasks (22%).

Picture a marketing executive at a mid-sized UK retailer, up against a Friday deadline, with no company AI licence issued yet. They paste a client's campaign brief into a personal ChatGPT account to get a first draft moving. Nothing about that decision feels risky in the moment. Deloitte's numbers suggest a version of that scene is playing out in roughly one in five conversations UK employees have with generative AI.

Why do UK employees turn to unauthorised AI tools?

Mostly, it's a gap where a sanctioned tool should be. Staff have deadlines now and a policy document doesn't meet them.

BCC and Atos's Powering Productivity survey (March 2026) found 70% of UK businesses give staff access to some AI tool, but only 48% set aside time for people to actually learn it. Ninety-seven per cent of UK organisations report at least one significant AI skills gap. Only one in five people in work feel confident using AI at all.

Confidence and access aren't the same thing. Someone handed a tool with no training, on a deadline, will reach for whatever gets the job done, sanctioned or not.

The other driver is an outright gap in policy. CIPD's guidance on preparing an organisation for AI use reports that a quarter of UK organisations don't allow generative AI and have no plans to change that. Staff at those firms aren't waiting. They're bringing their own.

How available AI is also depends heavily on where someone works. CIPD's research found generative AI enabled in 73% of public sector organisations, against 57% of private-sector employers and 62% in the third sector, with smaller private employers consistently more cautious than large ones. Where the door is closed formally, staff tend to open a different one themselves.

That uneven landscape sits against a backdrop of AI use spreading fast across the whole UK workforce, not just among staff already comfortable with it. Our wider look at the UK workforce evidence on AI and jobs covers that broader trend.

What are the risks of shadow AI for a UK employer?

The risk isn't the technology. It's that nobody in the business can see what's being typed into it, or where that data ends up.

Data protection and UK GDPR risk

The ICO defines a personal data breach as unauthorised access to, or disclosure of, personal data, and a breach likely to risk individuals' rights must be reported within 72 hours. Pasting a customer's name, address or order history into a consumer AI account with no data processing terms in place can meet that definition.

Our guide to what the ICO's guidance means for AI and UK GDPR covers the wider regulatory picture.

High-risk AI processing also needs a Data Protection Impact Assessment, something the ICO's own guidance on putting an AI policy in place sets out as a core reason to have one. Shadow use happens entirely outside that process.

Confidentiality and contract risk

A junior account handler pasting a client's draft campaign brief into a free AI tool to tidy the wording isn't malicious. It's still a potential breach of the confidentiality clause in that client's contract, and the business often won't know it happened until the client asks.

Security and shadow IT risk

Personal AI accounts sit outside single sign-on, outside the company's data retention rules, and outside any audit log IT can pull. If someone leaves the business, whatever they put into their personal ChatGPT account leaves with them, unrecoverable and unreviewable.

None of this requires bad intent. It takes an ordinary employee doing an ordinary task with the fastest tool to hand, which is exactly why a policy built on the assumption of good faith works better than one built on the assumption of wrongdoing.

What should a shadow AI policy actually include?

Five elements cover most of it. None of them need to be long.

  1. An approved tools list. Name the AI tools staff can use, what each is for, and who to ask for something new. A blank policy with no named alternative just sends people back to whatever they were already using.
  2. Clear data handling rules. State plainly what can and can't go into an AI tool: customer data, personal data, unpublished financials, source code, anything under an NDA. Specific examples beat a general instruction to "use good judgement".
  3. A no-blame reporting route. A named person or inbox for staff to flag AI tools they're already using, without it triggering disciplinary action. You can't fix usage you don't know about.
  4. A training commitment. Match the tools list with actual time to learn them. BCC's data shows the gap between access (70%) and training time (48%) is exactly where shadow use grows.
  5. A review date. Put a quarterly review in the policy itself. New tools appear faster than most policies get updated, and a policy with no review date is already out of date.

How do you roll this out without pushing AI further underground?

A ban announced without an alternative just teaches staff to hide what they're doing. The rollout matters as much as the policy text.

Start with an amnesty, not an audit. Ask people what they're already using and why, and don't discipline anyone for answering honestly. That single step surfaces most of the shadow use in a business within a fortnight.

Then name a sanctioned option for the three uses Microsoft's data shows are already happening: drafting messages, building reports, and finance-adjacent tasks. If there's no approved tool for a job staff are already doing, they'll keep using an unapproved one.

Finally, brief people rather than just publishing a document. A fifteen-minute team session explaining the policy and the reporting route lands better than a link in an all-staff email nobody opens.

A useful middle step for a larger organisation: pilot the policy with the highest-risk team first, often finance or an account-handling team with regular client data exposure, before rolling it out business-wide. Fixing confusing wording with fifteen people costs far less than fixing it once the whole company is already reading it.

Track whether shadow use actually falls afterwards, not just whether the tools list got read. Repeating the same self-reported usage question from the amnesty each quarter is the simplest way to see whether the approved tools are closing the gap or just adding to the list of things staff ignore.

How does a shadow AI policy differ from a full AI governance framework?

A shadow AI policy is the fast, narrow fix. A governance framework is the wider system it eventually sits inside.

DimensionShadow AI usage policyFull AI governance framework
ScopeAcceptable use of GenAI tools by any staff memberAll AI use across the business, including vendor and customer-facing systems
OwnerIT and HR, jointlyA named executive sponsor plus a cross-functional AI group
Review cycleQuarterlyAnnually, with quarterly check-ins
Core outputA one-to-two page acceptable use policy and an approved tools listA DPIA process, a risk register, and a sign-off route for new AI use cases
Typical first stepList what's already being used unofficiallyAppoint the executive sponsor and complete a DPIA on the highest-risk use case

Most UK SMEs need the policy first. It's achievable in a week and closes the biggest gap immediately. The lightest AI governance framework for a UK SME is the natural next step once the policy is in place, particularly once AI use moves beyond individual staff typing into a chat window and into systems that make decisions on their own.

The two aren't a straight upgrade path from one to the other. A business can run a solid shadow AI policy indefinitely if its AI use stays limited to staff drafting and summarising in approved chat tools. The trigger for the fuller framework is usually a specific decision, an AI tool starting to score candidates, flag transactions, or set prices, rather than a fixed date on a roadmap.

What should IT and HR do this quarter?

Applying Deloitte's shadow-AI usage rate to the UK's employment total gives a sense of scale. Deloitte found 19.5% of UK workers use shadow AI: 63% who knowingly use GenAI, multiplied by the 31% of those who do so without telling their employer.

Applied to the ONS's 34.48 million people in UK employment (May to July 2026), that's roughly 6.7 million UK workers routinely using AI tools their employer hasn't sanctioned (Tom & Co analysis of Deloitte and ONS data, September 2026).

  1. Run the amnesty first. Find out what's already in use before writing a word of policy.
  2. Name at least one approved tool per common use case. A policy with no alternative just gets ignored.
  3. Write the five-element policy above. One to two pages, reviewed quarterly, is enough to start.
  4. Check whether any use so far needs reporting. If personal or customer data has already gone into an unsanctioned tool, that's a conversation for whoever handles data protection, not something to leave until the next audit.
  5. Book the next review date now. Put it in a calendar before the policy goes out, not after.
