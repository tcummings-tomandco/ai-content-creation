Why do most AI projects fail, and is 95% actually true?

The widely quoted claim that 95% of AI projects fail comes from a July 2025 MIT study, and it means something narrower than it sounds: 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact, not that the projects collapsed. In the UK, the same divide shows up as breadth without depth. AI adoption almost tripled between late 2023 and 2026, while the average depth of use barely moved, according to the Office for National Statistics.

What does the data actually show about AI project failure?

MIT's Project NANDA produced the number everyone quotes. Its July 2025 report reviewed more than 300 public AI deployments, ran 52 structured interviews and collected 153 survey responses between January and June 2025. It found that 95% of generative AI pilots delivered no measurable profit-and-loss impact, against an estimated $30 to $40 billion in enterprise investment. Just 5% of pilots were extracting millions in value.

RAND Corporation got to a similar place from a different angle. Its August 2024 study interviewed 65 data scientists and engineers, each with at least five years' industry experience, and put the failure rate at more than 80%, twice the rate for non-AI IT projects. The leading cause wasn't the technology. Decisions made by business leadership caused most of the failures RAND documented, with data quality a close second.

Here's the bit that actually matters: "95% fail" and "80% fail" are measuring different things. MIT counted pilots with no profit-and-loss impact yet. RAND counted projects that missed their own stated goals. Neither is counting the number of AI projects that actually collapsed or lost real money, which is a smaller, harder number that nobody has published.

That distinction matters more in the UK than the headline suggests, because the UK isn't behind on trying AI. It's behind on the same thing everyone else is behind on: turning a working pilot into something that survives contact with a real workflow, a real budget cycle, and a real line manager asking what it actually did this quarter.

Why does the "95% fail" headline get the story wrong?

Three things get flattened every time this number gets repeated on a conference slide.

  1. It treats "no impact yet" as "failed forever." MIT's own report doesn't say generative AI doesn't work. It says most pilots haven't had the integration, feedback loops or workflow redesign that separate the 5% that scale from the 95% that stall. Enterprise software has followed this slow-then-fast curve before. Judging a three-year-old technology category on eighteen-month pilots was always going to produce a scary number.
  2. It skips the parts that are working. McKinsey's 2025 global AI survey found 88% of organisations now use AI in at least one function, up from 78% a year earlier. Adoption is real. Scaling it into the bottom line is the rare part: only 7% call AI fully scaled, and just 39% attribute any enterprise-level profit impact to it. That's a different problem to "AI doesn't work."
  3. It confuses total spend with money lost. MIT's $30 to $40 billion figure is enterprise-wide GenAI investment, not write-offs. Most of that money bought infrastructure, data cleanup and internal know-how the next pilot needs, which is closer to a down payment than a loss.

The UK's own numbers make the same point, from an entirely different data set.

What does the UK's own AI adoption data reveal?

The Office for National Statistics tracks this directly. Its Artificial intelligence in UK businesses: 2023 to 2026 release, published 20 July 2026, found self-reported AI use among businesses with 10 or more employees rose from around 12% in late 2023 to around 35% in 2026. Adoption is highest in information and communication (58%) and lowest in construction (13%).

Depth didn't keep pace with breadth. The average number of AI technologies used per adopting business rose only from about 1.4 to about 1.6 over the same period, per the same ONS release. Businesses turned AI on. Very few of them turned it up.

Tom & Co analysis: UK AI adoption breadth grew roughly 2.5 times faster than depth of use between late 2023 and 2026, based on the ONS's own figures (adoption 12% to 35%, a 2.9x rise; depth 1.4 to 1.6 tools per business, a 1.14x rise; 2.9 ÷ 1.14 ≈ 2.5). That's the UK's own "GenAI Divide": wide trial, shallow follow-through.

The British Chambers of Commerce found the same pattern from the SME side. Its Future of Work: AI in the Workplace report, run with the University of Essex and Atos and published in March 2026, put SME adoption at 54% in its January 2026 wave, up from 23% in 2023. Ninety-five percent of those AI-using SMEs said it had made no difference to headcount, and 86% said job roles hadn't changed.

Government isn't immune to the same divide, and it has the data to prove it. The National Audit Office's Use of artificial intelligence in government report (15 March 2024) surveyed 89 government bodies and found 37% actively using AI, 25% piloting it and 11% planning to. Its warning: there's no systematic way of sharing what's been learned across all that pilot activity.

That warning aged well. In July 2026, the NAO's Government workforce planning: lessons learned report pushed back on the government's own claim that AI and digital transformation could save £45 billion a year, telling departments to verify the number first. Parliament's Science, Innovation and Technology Committee, per Computer Weekly's reporting, had already called the same figure "worryingly optimistic". (Public and private sectors, running the identical experiment: counting AI's value before it's banked.)

Which AI projects actually succeed, and what do they have in common?

MIT's report didn't only measure failure. It looked at what the successful 5% did differently, and three patterns showed up repeatedly.

Buy before you build

Tools bought from external vendors reached deployment at roughly twice the rate of tools built in-house, which more often failed on brittleness or a poor fit with how people actually worked. Building your own large language model application sounds more strategic. It usually isn't the faster route to value, and it's rarely the cheaper one either once a specialist has to maintain it.

Go narrow, not broad

The pilots that scaled were rarely pitched as "AI for the whole business." They picked one high-frequency, well-defined task, drafting a specific report, triaging a specific inbox, and let the tool get properly good at exactly that. A UK professional services firm running one AI tool well on client-reporting drafts has a better shot at MIT's 5% than one running five half-adopted tools everywhere.

Decentralise ownership, keep accountability

The organisations that made pilots stick handed day-to-day control to the team using the tool, while keeping one named owner accountable for the result. Centrally run pilots with no obvious owner were the ones most likely to quietly stop being used. The BCC's own finding fits: UK adoption is fastest among larger SMEs and B2B professional services firms, slowest in manufacturing and consumer-facing sectors.

What should a UK leader do differently this quarter?

  1. Pick one narrow use case, not a portfolio. Every pattern in this piece (vendor tools, narrow scope, a named owner) points the same way. Resist the urge to run five pilots at once. Run one properly.
  2. Buy first, build only where you genuinely must. Reserve in-house builds for the handful of workflows where nothing off the shelf fits, and treat that as the harder, slower option, not the impressive one. If cost is the blocker, UK SMEs can apply for Innovate UK's BridgeAI programme before paying full price for a custom build.
  3. Name an accountable owner before the pilot starts. Not "the innovation team". One person, with their name against the result, who answers for it in three months' time.
  4. Set an integration date, not just a trial period. MIT's own finding is that pilots stall on integration and feedback loops, not on the model. Put a date on when the tool needs to be wired into a real workflow, rather than just tested next to one.
  5. Agree what "success" looks like before you start. A baseline number and a target number, written down, before the pilot begins. Without that, nobody can honestly say afterwards whether it worked.

How do you tell if your AI project is failing or just early?

Four quick checks, borrowed directly from what separated MIT's 5% from its 95%.

  1. Has anyone outside the pilot team used it in the past fortnight without being asked to?
  2. Is there a named person accountable for the result, or is it "the team's" project?
  3. Has the tool been properly wired into a real workflow, or is it still a side tab open next to the real system?
  4. Could you state, in one sentence and one number, what this pilot has saved or earned so far?

If the honest answer to most of these is no, this isn't a failed AI project. It's an ordinary one that hasn't yet been given the conditions MIT and RAND both found scaling actually needs.
