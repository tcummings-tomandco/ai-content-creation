What did the Getty v Stability AI ruling decide for UK copyright?

In November 2025, the High Court largely rejected Getty Images' copyright and trademark claims against Stability AI ([2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch)), but only on narrow technical grounds: Getty couldn't prove AI training happened in the UK, and the court found AI model weights aren't a legal "copy". Getty has permission to appeal. UK copyright law itself hasn't changed, and the government's March 2026 report offers no new AI training exception.

What did the Getty v Stability AI ruling actually decide?

Getty Images sued Stability AI in the High Court, arguing that Stable Diffusion, Stability's image generation model, had been trained on millions of Getty's copyrighted photographs without a licence. Getty pointed to outputs carrying a garbled version of its own watermark as evidence its library had gone into the training set.

Judgment landed on 4 November 2025, in Getty Images (US) Inc & Ors v Stability AI Ltd [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), after a trial that had run since 2023.

Getty's strongest claim, that training the model itself infringed UK copyright, never actually got decided. Shortly before closing submissions, Getty dropped its primary copyright and database right claims. It couldn't show the training and development of Stable Diffusion took place in the UK, so a UK court had no jurisdiction over that underlying question.

That left a narrower secondary infringement claim: had Stability imported an "infringing copy" of Getty's works into the UK by making the trained model available here? The court said no. A trained AI model doesn't store or reproduce the images it learned from.

It contains statistically trained parameters, not a recognisable copy of any photograph, so it isn't "copying" in the sense section 17 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 requires. Put plainly: the model's weights are billions of numbers describing statistical patterns, not a stored photo library it can play back on demand.

Getty didn't come away empty-handed. The court found narrow, historic trademark infringement under sections 10(1) and 10(2) of the Trade Marks Act 1994: early versions of Stable Diffusion sometimes generated images carrying a distorted Getty Images watermark. A wider claim under section 10(3) was dismissed.

The finding is limited to older Stable Diffusion versions (v1.x and v2.x); it doesn't touch the models Stability actually sells today.

Getty was ordered to pay 69.4% of Stability's legal costs, with an interim payment on account of £4,395,656.89, following the consequentials hearing on 16 and 17 December 2025.

Getty isn't done. At that same hearing, the judge granted Getty permission to appeal in full on the secondary infringement point: whether an AI model needs to store an actual copy of a work to count as an "infringing copy" at all. Until the Court of Appeal rules, that specific legal question stays open.

Does the ruling mean AI training is now legal under UK copyright law?

No, and that's the most common misreading doing the rounds since November 2025. Coverage that reduced the judgment to "Stability wins, so AI training is legal" skipped over what the court actually did, and didn't, decide.

The training-infringement question, the one every UK AI developer actually cares about, was never ruled on. Getty abandoned that claim for lack of UK jurisdiction, not because a judge found training on copyrighted images lawful. The point remains untested in a UK court.

UK copyright law hasn't moved either. Section 29A of the CDPA 1988 still permits text and data mining only for non-commercial research. Training a commercial model on copyrighted UK works without a licence, and without that narrow exception applying, is in principle still an infringement. Stability won this case because Getty couldn't prove where the training happened, not because the underlying law changed.

The one point the court did decide, that an AI model's weights aren't themselves an infringing "copy", is now the subject of Getty's appeal. A Court of Appeal ruling could still go the other way.

Take a UK marketing agency that switched to an AI image generator for campaign visuals this year, reasoning that "the Getty case settled it". That reasoning doesn't hold up. Nothing in the judgment says training UK-based models on scraped copyrighted images is safe, only that Getty couldn't win on the specific facts it brought to a London court.

What copyright protection applies to content you generate using AI in the UK?

Getty v Stability AI was about training data going in. The other live question for most UK businesses is what comes out: who, if anyone, owns the copyright in something an AI tool generates for you.

Content with no real human input

UK copyright protects original works with a human author. Content an AI tool produces from a bare, generic prompt, with no meaningful human creative choices shaping the result, generally isn't protected by copyright at all. Anyone could, in principle, reuse it freely.

Content shaped by significant human input

Section 9(3) of the CDPA 1988 covers "computer-generated works" made "in circumstances such that there is no human author". It treats the person who made the arrangements necessary for the work's creation as the author. Detailed prompting, deliberate selection, and substantial editing all strengthen the case that a human made those arrangements.

Computer-generated works get a shorter term too: 50 years from creation under section 12(7), not the usual life of the author plus 70 years. Nobody has definitively tested how section 9(3) applies to a modern generative AI system in a UK court, so treat it as a reasonable legal argument, not a guarantee.

Content built on unlicensed training data

A separate risk sits underneath ownership: did the tool that generated your content train on copyrighted material without a licence? Getty v Stability AI shows how hard that is to prove and to litigate, but hard to prove isn't the same as risk-free for a business relying on the output commercially.

A UK retailer publishing AI-generated product photography, for instance, owns very little legal certainty on either front. It may not own copyright in the images it publishes, and it can't easily verify what the tool that made them was trained on. Both gaps are worth closing before the output goes anywhere near a live campaign.

Is a UK text and data mining exception for AI training coming?

The government ran a formal consultation on copyright and AI from 17 December 2024 to 25 February 2025, and received 11,520 responses from creators, rights holders, AI developers and researchers, according to the government's own Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence.

Its original preferred option, a broad text and data mining exception with a rightsholder opt-out, was unpopular with creative industries respondents. By the time the statutory report landed, that option was off the table. The other policy options the consultation put forward, ranging from making no legislative change to requiring AI developers to licence training data outright, remain on the table, with the government stating no preference between them.

Tom & Co analysis of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025's own statutory deadline: the government's report was due within nine months of royal assent on 19 June 2025, a deadline falling on 19 March 2026 under sections 135 and 136. It published the report on 18 March 2026, one day inside its own deadline.

The report's headline conclusion is that there's no preferred policy option right now. The government says it wants more evidence and more international alignment before it legislates either a new exception or new obligations on AI developers.

On transparency, over 90% of consultation respondents wanted AI developers to disclose what they trained models on. The government agrees that's the right instinct, but confirmed no plan to actually require it in UK law. For now, transparency stays a voluntary ask, not a legal one.

A companion economic impact assessment, published alongside the report under section 135 of the same Act, weighs the same four policy options in pounds and pence rather than principle. Neither document commits the government to a timetable for actually legislating, which is the detail UK businesses planning around this should sit with.

What should a UK business do about AI and copyright right now?

None of this needs a specialist IP lawyer on retainer to start closing off. Five practical steps cover most of the exposure a UK business actually carries from using AI tools for content and imagery today.

  1. Get training-data provenance in writing. Ask any AI vendor whether the model was trained on licensed data, and get the answer in the contract, not a sales call, before you rely on its output commercially.
  2. Don't assume AI-generated content is automatically yours to protect. A bare prompt with no real editing is unlikely to attract copyright at all under current UK law.
  3. Keep a record of your own creative input. Prompting decisions, selection choices, and edits are the evidence that supports a section 9(3) argument if you ever need to make one.
  4. Watch the appeal, not just the headline. The Court of Appeal's ruling on whether model weights can be an "infringing copy" could change the practical risk picture for UK AI developers.
  5. Treat "no exception yet" as the current baseline. Budget for licensing costs on training data now rather than assuming a new UK exception will arrive to remove that cost later.

Pick one AI tool your business already relies on for content or imagery, and ask the vendor the training-data question directly this week. Getty v Stability AI shows how expensive it gets to find out the answer only when a court asks it for you.
